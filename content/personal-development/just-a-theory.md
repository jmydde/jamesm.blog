Some ideas take years to actually arrive at. You read something, it doesn't quite land, you park it. A different book, a different conversation, a different bad night of insomnia scrolling through arguments you don't fully follow, and a piece clicks into place that wasn't there before. Repeat that for a decade or two and you end up holding a view on something like the simulation hypothesis, or whether consciousness is fundamental to reality rather than something the brain manufactures, that has real scaffolding underneath it - half-remembered papers, false starts, positions you used to hold and later dropped.

None of that scaffolding survives being explained in a few minutes. And a few minutes is usually all you get.

## The compression problem

Try explaining a theory like that to someone at a dinner table, or on a call, or in the ten minutes before a meeting starts. What comes out is not "here is a view I've been slowly building for years, subject to revision, held loosely because I know I could be wrong." What comes out is one sentence - *I think there's a real chance we're in a simulation* - stripped of every qualifier, every "here's why I don't think that's as crazy as it sounds," every "obviously nobody actually knows."

That sentence, on its own, sounds like a claim. A verdict. Something to be accepted or rejected on the spot. It isn't one. It's the compressed output of a much longer, much more tentative process, and the compression is lossy in exactly the way that matters: it throws away the doubt and keeps only the headline.

I have written elsewhere on this blog about [how likely it actually is that we're living in a simulation](/theories/how-likely-are-we-living-in-a-simulation/), and my honest answer is that nobody knows, and the question may not even have a clean numerical answer. That gap - between "nobody knows" and a flat verdict either way - is where I try to actually live with these ideas. But conveying that gap in conversation takes far longer than conveying the headline, and most conversations don't have the room for it.

## A lot of people aren't open to hearing it anyway

Even when there is room, the compression problem is only half of it. The other half is that plenty of people have already decided, before hearing the specifics, which category an idea belongs to - and simulation theory, consciousness as fundamental, the multiverse, anything that sounds like it belongs to "fringe physics" gets filed as not worth their time before the actual reasoning behind it gets a hearing.

A few things seem to do that work.

One is straightforward ego. Some people experience being wrong, or even just being uncertain, as a kind of loss - a small defeat to be avoided rather than a normal part of thinking. Psychologists call the broader pattern [motivated reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivated_reasoning): the tendency to process new information in whatever direction protects a conclusion you were already committed to, rather than in whatever direction the evidence points. It doesn't feel like bias from the inside. It feels like being right.

A second is identity. The Yale researcher Dan Kahan and colleagues at the [Cultural Cognition Project](https://www.culturalcognition.net/) have spent years documenting something related but distinct: people don't just defend conclusions, they defend group membership. Accepting an idea that your friends, your worldview, or your sense of yourself as a sensible, grounded person would reject can feel like a small act of disloyalty to your own identity, regardless of whether the idea is actually true. The more capable and informed someone is, in some of Kahan's findings, the more polarised they can become on identity-charged topics - not despite their reasoning ability, but because they're better equipped to defend the position they started with.

A third, and the one I think gets underestimated most, is that some people simply aren't comfortable holding an idea as a hypothesis. A theory, properly understood, isn't a belief you've adopted - it's a claim you're testing, something held loosely that you'd happily discard if the evidence went the other way. That's a genuinely different mental posture from "this is true" or "this is false," and not everyone finds it a natural place to stand. If a statement can only be processed as accepted or rejected, with no third setting for "interesting, let's see," then any unfamiliar idea arrives as a demand rather than an invitation.

The writer and forecaster Julia Galef has a useful frame for this in [*The Scout Mindset*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scout_Mindset): most of us default to what she calls a soldier mindset when a belief feels threatened, defending territory rather than mapping it. A scout, by contrast, is just trying to see what's actually there. The two postures produce completely different responses to the exact same sentence - one hears an attack to repel, the other hears a claim worth checking.

## The instant verdict

The tell, when it happens, is speed. A genuinely considered disagreement takes a moment - it engages with what was actually proposed, picks at a specific weak point, asks a follow-up question. An instant verdict does none of that. It arrives before there's been time to take in what was said, and it's a response to the *category* the idea belongs to, not to its content.

That's the real cost of the compression problem meeting a closed door: the four-minute version of a decades-long theory gets judged as if it were the whole argument, by someone who has already decided the category is beneath consideration, and the actual reasoning - the part that took years to build - never gets a hearing at all.

## A theory is not a verdict

Most of what I write about on this blog under [theories](/theories/) and in the more speculative corners of [AI](/ai/) - simulation, the [multiverse](/theories/huge-numbers/), whether [consciousness might be fundamental rather than produced by the brain](/ai/donald-hoffman-consciousness-fundamental/) - I try hard to hold as exactly that: theories, always evolving, and I'm genuinely happy to be proven wrong on any of them. I'm not a physicist. I'm a hobbyist who finds this stuff fascinating and reads around it in my own time, nothing more credentialed than that. On the consciousness question specifically I'm honestly still of two minds - drawn to the idea that consciousness could be fundamental rather than computational, but not settled on it either way. I revise these views often, sometimes within a week, because holding a position loosely enough that new information can move it is, as far as I can tell, the actual mechanism by which anyone gets closer to something true.

Sharing that kind of view with someone who can only receive it as a claim to be accepted or rejected is asking them to do something that took years to build as a habit: hold a thought without immediately deciding what to do with it. That's not a small ask, even though it feels like one from the outside.

This isn't unique to physics-adjacent ideas, either. The same reflex is a close cousin of something I wrote about in [The Quiet Discipline of Self-Honesty](/personal-development/the-quiet-discipline-of-self-honesty/) - the mind's habit of protecting an existing self-image over updating it with better information. Simulation theory and self-honesty look like unrelated topics. The muscle that resists both is the same one.

## Is it worth trying to explain?

Not always, and I've made peace with that. Not every idea needs every audience, and it isn't anyone's job to convert someone who isn't looking to be moved. Some of it is picking the right moment - a theory that took a decade to build rarely survives being unpacked in the ten minutes before someone has to leave. And some of it is being honest that pushing past an instant verdict is exhausting in a way that usually isn't worth it unless there's a real chance the other person will actually let the idea in rather than just wait for a gap to disagree.

But when someone does stay with it past the first reflexive no - when the questions shift from defending a position to actually mapping the idea - that's the part worth the friction. It suggests the resistance was never really about the content in the first place. It was about being asked to consider something before deciding how to feel about it. Once that stops feeling like a threat, curiosity tends to do the rest of the work on its own.
