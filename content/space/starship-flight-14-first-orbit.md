## TL;DR

- **Starship Flight 14** lifted off from Starbase at **8:49 a.m. EDT on 28 September** and reached orbit for the first time. The first thirteen flights were flown on purpose short of orbital speed
- One of the upper stage's six Raptors shut down during ascent. Controllers nearly skipped the insertion burn, then went ahead. A single engine fired and put the ship into an orbit of about **275 km**
- The ship deployed **26 Starlink V3 satellites**, the first ones to stay in orbit. SpaceX says each adds about **1 Tbps**, roughly ten times a Falcon 9 Starlink, and that it has contact with all 26
- The plan was about six orbits and a splashdown west of Chile nearly ten hours after launch. After the engine problem, SpaceX cut the flight to a pre-planned zone in the northern Pacific. Splashdown was at **11:57 a.m. EDT**, north of Hawaii
- The ship reached the water under control, then broke up. The FAA says the flight stayed inside its authorised plan and will not require a mishap investigation

For thirteen flights, SpaceX stopped Starship short of orbit on purpose. [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/09/starships-first-orbital-launch-gives-lift-to-spacexs-next-gen-starlinks/) put the reason cleanly: a suborbital arc is passively safe. If the ship dies after the engines cut off, it is already on a path back into the atmosphere, over an empty stretch of ocean. Orbit is the opposite. Once you are up, something has to fire again to bring you down, or the vehicle comes back when it feels like it.

Monday's flight was the first time SpaceX chose the second option. It also showed why they had waited.

## The burn they almost skipped

Liftoff was 8:49 a.m. EDT, 7:49 a.m. in Texas, from Pad 2 at Starbase. [Ars](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/09/starships-first-orbital-launch-gives-lift-to-spacexs-next-gen-starlinks/) and [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/09/28/starship-returns-to-earth-rocket-splashes-down-north-of-hawaii-after-three-hour-flight/) agree on the clock time. Thirty-three Raptors on the Super Heavy booster, six on the ship, a vehicle 124 metres tall.

The ascent was the familiar Starship sequence until it wasn't. [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/09/28/starship-returns-to-earth-rocket-splashes-down-north-of-hawaii-after-three-hour-flight/) reports hot staging at two minutes and twenty seconds: the ship's engines lit, the booster fell away, flipped, and flew back toward the Texas coast. One booster engine had already quit on the way up. A second quit during the boostback burn. The booster still made a controlled vertical splashdown a few miles offshore. On the previous flight, a short boostback and a partial landing burn had ended in a hard splashdown. This return was the cleaner one.

The ship's problem was sharper. One of its six Raptors shut down early during the climb. The other five burned longer and still reached the planned suborbital coast, the point where, on every earlier flight, SpaceX would have been done with propulsion. Getting to orbit required a second burn.

For a few minutes it looked as if that burn would not happen. Dan Huot, commenting on SpaceX's webcast, first said mission managers were going to skip it. [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-megarocket-flight-14-orbital-launch-success) quotes him a moment later: "We are not giving up yet. We will continue to hang out." The failed engine was not one of the three centre engines needed to enter orbit and, later, to leave it. They cleared those three and lit one.

Huot, as quoted by Ars: "We were only going to go if we were very confident in the core systems that have to work to get you out of orbit once you're up there. We took some extra time, added some extra drama, but we were able to clear the three center engines and do our first ever orbital insertion burn."

The burn lasted 19 seconds and added about 204 mph, a little over one percent of the ship's speed. That was enough. Mission control called "Starship is orbital." Elon Musk posted, in [Space.com's](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-megarocket-flight-14-orbital-launch-success) account of the message, "First orbital flight of Starship successful!" The orbit was about 171 miles, 276 kilometres.

The caution was the whole point of the design. The failure they were avoiding, as Ars describes it, was a 100-ton vehicle stranded in orbit and falling back uncontrolled. Huot said they would only commit to orbit if they trusted the engines that had to light again at the end. They did, with one engine already written off.

## Twenty-six satellites that only this rocket can carry

Orbit was the ticket to the payload. The ship released 26 Starlink V3 satellites from a dispenser Ars describes as a pulley-and-cable rig, the "Pez" system, over a stretch of the pass that included a view over South Africa.

These are not the dummy satellites from earlier tests. [Ars](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/09/starships-first-orbital-launch-gives-lift-to-spacexs-next-gen-starlinks/) notes that Flight 13, in July, also threw V3s overboard, on a suborbital arc, and they burned up. Monday's are the first ones meant to stay. SpaceX says it established contact with all 26.

The satellites are why Starship's orbit matters commercially, not just as a test box ticked. Ars, citing SpaceX, says each V3 weighs about two tonnes, will not fit in a Falcon 9 fairing, and is built to add about 1 terabit per second to the network, on the order of ten times an older Starlink. A future ship might carry 60. This one carried 26, into a low-inclination orbit, roughly 30 degrees either side of the equator, because the Texas range still restricts flights over populated land.

Caleb Henry of Quilty Space, on a Spaceflight Now broadcast quoted by Ars, said the satellites had been finished and waiting in Texas because the rocket was the late part. He also said customers should not expect the network to feel different yet. Capacity on orbit is not the same thing as enough satellites overhead to change the service.

Ars reports a related shift already visible on the manifest: SpaceX has no further Falcon 9 Starlink launches scheduled from Cape Canaveral. Polar Starlink flights on Falcon 9 from Vandenberg are still on the books. The big satellites have a single ride, and that ride flew for the first time on Monday.

## Three hours, not ten

The published plan was about six orbits and a splashdown west of Chile, nearly ten hours after liftoff. Spaceflight Now had the Chile splashdown around 6:40 p.m. EDT. That is not what flew.

SpaceX's own recap, quoted by Ars: "Out of an abundance of caution given the engine issue experienced during ascent, the flight control team decided to limit the duration spent on orbit and proceeded with de-orbiting Starship to a splashdown location in the northern Pacific Ocean. This area was one of two pre-coordinated landing zones identified before launch that was cleared of sea and air traffic."

The deorbit burn worked. The ship came in over the Pacific, heat shield facing temperatures Ars puts at up to 2,600°F (1,430°C), belly first, then flipped upright for a landing burn. Splashdown was 11:57 a.m. EDT, three hours and eight minutes after launch, north of Hawaii, in view of a buoy SpaceX had put on the backup zone.

Then it broke up. [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/09/28/starship-returns-to-earth-rocket-splashes-down-north-of-hawaii-after-three-hour-flight/) describes a controlled tail-first arrival, followed by the ship tipping onto its side and exploding as leftover propellant ignited. Ars says this flight did not repeat the intact splashdown of the July test, and that SpaceX expects most water landings to end in a fireball. Both descriptions fit the same sequence: the landing burn did its job, and the vehicle did not survive the sea the way July's did.

An FAA spokesperson told Ars: "All flight events for both the Starship vehicle and the Super Heavy booster occurred within the scope of planned and FAA authorized activities." There will not be a formal mishap investigation. Ending early, in a zone cleared before launch, was inside the plan. It was also an admission that the engine they lost on the way up was enough reason not to loiter.

NASA administrator Jared Isaacman posted, as quoted by Spaceflight Now: "Gorgeous launch, getting Ship to orbit and managing every step in a safe, responsible, and especially inspirational way." The interesting half of that sentence is "managing every step." The step they managed was the decision not to stay.

## What orbit does not yet prove

Reaching orbit is the milestone the previous thirteen flights were built to approach. It is not the milestone [Artemis](/space/artemis-iii-lander-architecture/) is waiting on.

A lunar landing still needs tanker flights, propellant transferred in orbit, a ship that can be caught and flown again, and a heat shield that survives often enough to make that routine. None of those flew on Monday. SpaceX has caught the Super Heavy booster with the tower arms on earlier tests. It has not tried that with the ship, and both the booster and the ship splashed down in the ocean this time. Ars says the next items on the list are a longer stay in orbit, a ship catch back at Starbase, and the first in-orbit refuelling demonstration.

The engine that forced the short flight is not a one-off in Ars's telling. This was the third Starship flight on Raptor 3 engines, and Ars says each of the three has had an engine problem: a premature shutdown on a May flight, engines that failed to light on the pad in July (they swapped two and launched a week later), and Monday's shutdown. Musk wrote on X, as quoted by Ars, that Raptor 3 "greatly reduces mass and leak paths, but makes it harder to replace some parts," and that "deleting parts sounds easy in theory" and "requires many iterations." The inside of the engine, he wrote, "has very complex geometry that can only be manufactured using highly modified 3D metal printing."

So the honest score from Monday is specific. First orbit. First Starlink V3s that get to stay there. A booster that came back more cleanly than last time. A ship that could still deorbit after losing an engine, because the engine it lost was not the one it needed. And a flight three times longer than the old suborbital arcs that was still seven hours short of the one they had filed.

## What I'm watching

**Whether the next flight is actually in October.** SpaceX has not announced a date. Ars says an FAA air-traffic planning schedule this week pointed at another Starbase launch in October. A schedule entry is not a commitment.

**Florida.** Spaceflight Now says the first Starship launch from the Cape is planned for late this year or early next, with pads at Kennedy and Cape Canaveral still under construction. A second range is what turns a Texas test cadence into something that can fly the higher-inclination orbits the Starlink constellation actually wants.

**Whether Raptor 3 stops shedding engines.** Three flights, three propulsion snags, and the third one is the reason this orbit lasted an afternoon. The insertion burn can keep succeeding while the engine remains the schedule.

**The catch, and the tanker.** Orbit without refuelling does not get a crew to the Moon. That dependency is the one I wrote about in [the Artemis lander piece](/space/artemis-iii-lander-architecture/). Flight 14 removed the excuse that Starship had never been in orbit. It did not remove the flights that still have to happen after this one.
