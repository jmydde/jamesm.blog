For sixteen years Demis Hassabis ran the lab he co-founded. On 5 August 2026 he stopped. In a [note to Google DeepMind staff](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/message-ceo/next-chapter-ai-momentum/) he wrote that he had "been working towards AGI my whole life and now, like many of you, I feel it is close at hand", and that he was handing over his day-to-day operational responsibilities so he could "focus on the big picture". He became Chair of Google DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet. Koray Kavukcuoglu, his colleague of more than thirteen years, took over the lab. The interesting question is no longer how Hassabis runs DeepMind. It is what the only Nobel laureate among the frontier-lab founders does once he is no longer running one.

## TL;DR

- **On 5 August 2026 Hassabis stepped down as CEO of Google DeepMind** to become its Chair and Alphabet's Chief Scientist. He [remains CEO of Isomorphic Labs](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/message-ceo/next-chapter-ai-momentum/). Koray Kavukcuoglu now leads the lab as SVP of Google DeepMind.
- The move came at the end of a rough summer. John Jumper, who shared the Nobel with Hassabis, [left for Anthropic in June](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/20/nobel-laureate-john-jumper-is-leaving-deepmind-for-rival-anthropic/). In July the FT reported that DeepMind had [dissolved the dedicated AlphaFold team](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-google-deepmind-broke-up-the-alphafold-team/). Google had also delayed Gemini 3.5 Pro, and Jeff Dean left on the same day Hassabis changed roles.
- His AGI timeline has compressed. In January 2026 he was still saying [five to 10 years](https://fortune.com/2026/01/23/deepmind-demis-hassabis-anthropic-dario-amodei-yann-lecun-ai-davos/). By July he was writing that AGI is ["probably only a few short years away"](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/a-framework-for-frontier-ai-and-the-dawning-of-a-new-age/).
- His main policy proposal is a US frontier-AI standards body modelled on FINRA. Labs would submit models voluntarily for review up to 30 days before release, and passing would later become mandatory for the US market.
- On 12 September he [backed Dario Amodei's call to pace the frontier](https://disputeindex.com/events/528-demis-hassabis-endorsed-the-direction-of-amodei-s). Four days later he launched the [DeepMind Institute](https://thenextweb.com/news/deepmind-institute-legg-hassabis-agi-essays) with Shane Legg and James Manyika.
- The background is still remarkable. He reached chess master standard at 13, co-designed *Theme Park* at 17 and earned a PhD in cognitive neuroscience. He co-founded DeepMind in 2010 and sold it to Google for £400 million in 2014. He shared the [2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/press-release/) and was knighted the same year.

## The man behind the company

Hassabis's biography explains the shape of everything he has built, so it is worth getting right.

He learned chess at four and reached [master standard at 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demis_Hassabis), with an Elo rating of 2,300. Cambridge asked him to take a gap year because he was so young, and he spent it at Bullfrog Productions, where at 17 he co-designed and lead-programmed *Theme Park* with Peter Molyneux. He then read Computer Science at Queens' College, Cambridge, and graduated with a double first in 1997. In 1998 he founded Elixir Studios, which shipped *Republic: The Revolution* and *Evil Genius*. *Republic* tried to simulate an entire fictional country and was scaled back from its original vision.

After Elixir he went back to academia and took a PhD in cognitive neuroscience at UCL in 2009, supervised by Eleanor Maguire. His first paper showed that patients with hippocampal amnesia could not imagine new experiences, and *Science* listed that work among its top ten breakthroughs of 2007. He was a visiting scientist at MIT and Harvard, then took a postdoctoral fellowship at UCL's Gatsby Computational Neuroscience Unit. That is where he met Shane Legg.

No other frontier-lab leader has this mix of serious chess player, commercial game designer and published neuroscientist. You can hear it in how he talks about intelligence. He treats it as a set of interacting capabilities the brain solves in ways we only partly understand, and he thinks it should be built by understanding it, not just by scaling it.

## From DeepMind to Google DeepMind

Hassabis co-founded DeepMind in 2010 with Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman, who now runs Microsoft AI. The mission was to "solve intelligence" and then use it "to solve everything else". Google bought the company in 2014 for £400 million, and DeepMind stayed a largely independent unit based in London.

The lab's defining results came before the generative-AI boom. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in Seoul in 2016. AlphaFold2 dominated the CASP protein-structure benchmark in 2020, and AlphaFold3 extended prediction to interactions with DNA, RNA and potential drugs in 2024. The [Nobel committee](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/press-release/) said AlphaFold2 had predicted the structure of virtually all of the 200 million known proteins and had been used by more than two million people in 190 countries. Hassabis made it free. That choice reportedly [caused friction inside Google](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-google-deepmind-broke-up-the-alphafold-team/), where the breakthrough brought prestige but little direct revenue.

In April 2023 Google [merged DeepMind with the Brain team](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/ai/april-ai-update/) from Google Research to form Google DeepMind, with Hassabis as CEO. The same announcement made Jeff Dean Google's Chief Scientist. Gemini is the product of that merger. For three years Hassabis ran both a frontier model programme on a product cadence and a science programme on a research cadence.

## The summer of 2026

The leadership change makes more sense once you line up what came before it.

**Talent left.** On 19 June John Jumper said he was leaving after "nearly 9 years" to join Anthropic. In his [farewell post](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/20/nobel-laureate-john-jumper-is-leaving-deepmind-for-rival-anthropic/) he credited Hassabis with taking "a real chance letting me lead the AlphaFold team just six months after finishing my PhD". Noam Shazeer left for OpenAI the same week.

**The AlphaFold team was broken up.** The FT reported in July that the dedicated team had been dissolved. Researchers were reassigned to Gemini work, enzyme design, genomics and fusion, or moved to Isomorphic Labs, and some followed Jumper out. A DeepMind spokesperson [told *Scientific American*](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-google-deepmind-broke-up-the-alphafold-team/) that many of those moves had happened more than a year earlier, and the public AlphaFold database and server remain available. CASP co-founder John Moult said the move was "not surprising": by the benchmark DeepMind chose, AlphaFold had done its job.

**The product clock got louder.** [Gizmodo reported](https://gizmodo.com/google-deepmind-boss-demis-hassabis-steps-down-from-ceo-role-2000794979) that Google had delayed Gemini 3.5 Pro, which had been expected in June. Bloomberg reported that the model had fallen short of some internal goals.

**Then the reshuffle.** On 5 August Sundar Pichai [announced](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/message-ceo/next-chapter-ai-momentum/) the new roles. "We have to accelerate all this work and stay focused on the AI frontier," he wrote. "At the same time, there's never been a more important moment to shape the future of AGI and science." He wrote that Hassabis "has described us as standing in the foothills of the singularity". The same day, Jeff Dean said he was leaving to start a public-benefit corporation called Discovery Loop, with Google as a founding investor.

Google's framing is that this gives Hassabis room to work on AGI strategy while Kavukcuoglu speeds up the model work. In his note, Hassabis said the Gemini models "are in good hands with Koray and the leads, as they have been for a while", and pointed to Gemini 4. I read it as a separation of jobs that had been pulling against each other since the 2023 merger. One job is shipping a frontier model every few months. The other is deciding what the arrival of AGI should look like. Google now has one person for each.

## What he says now

The biggest change in Hassabis's public message this year is the timeline.

**From five-to-ten years to a few years.** Hassabis said for years that AGI was roughly five to 10 years out, and he was still saying so at Davos in January 2026. There he told the audience that today's systems are "nowhere near" human-level AGI, and that ["maybe we need one or two more breakthroughs"](https://fortune.com/2026/01/23/deepmind-demis-hassabis-anthropic-dario-amodei-yann-lecun-ai-davos/). By April he was telling Sequoia that we are three-quarters of the way there. His July essay, [*A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age*](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/a-framework-for-frontier-ai-and-the-dawning-of-a-new-age/), opens with the sentence that AGI, "a system that exhibits all the cognitive capabilities the brain has, is probably only a few short years away". He repeated the "few short years" line at the King's summit at Dumfries House in September, where he also called the chance of something going wrong ["definitely non-zero"](https://thenextweb.com/news/king-charles-ai-summit-existential-dangers-hassabis-friar). His definition has not changed: all the cognitive capabilities of the human brain. What has changed is how close he thinks it is.

**A regulator modelled on Wall Street's.** The July essay is his most concrete policy proposal to date. It asks the US to set up a Standards Body "much like the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA)". It would be a federally overseen, mostly industry-funded organisation, with a board that includes independent technical experts and open-source representatives. As [TechCrunch summarised it](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/14/deepmind-ceo-calls-for-an-independent-standards-body-to-regulate-frontier-ai/), frontier labs would first share models voluntarily up to 30 days before release, and once the process had proved itself, passing would be required to deploy in the US. The essay also allows requirements to be "ratcheted up if the seriousness of the situation demands, including coordinating a slowdown in development among the Frontier Labs if deemed necessary".

**Agreeing with Amodei.** That clause mattered on 12 September, when Dario Amodei published *We Must Pace the Frontier*. Hassabis [quote-posted it](https://disputeindex.com/events/528-demis-hassabis-endorsed-the-direction-of-amodei-s) that day: "Dario's essay points towards the right path forward. The details need working through, but the direction is correct for meeting this critical moment. This is also why we recently put out our proposal for an industry-wide standards body for frontier AI." The two plans differ. Amodei wants evaluators embedded inside each lab, and Hassabis wants an external body. But two founders of rival labs now both say the pace needs managing.

**An institution for the argument.** On 16 September Google DeepMind launched the [DeepMind Institute](https://thenextweb.com/news/deepmind-institute-legg-hassabis-agi-essays), a platform for essays and research on AGI. Its directors are Legg, Hassabis and James Manyika. The launch essay names cybersecurity, biorisk and "the potential for loss of control in future self-improving systems" as concerns, and says its pieces "should not be read as Google's official view". Hassabis's framework essay is one of the first pieces on it. This is the clearest sign of what the chief scientist role is for: shaping the argument about AGI, not shipping the next model.

**Science first, still.** None of this has displaced the thesis he has held longest: the most valuable use of AI is accelerating science, and above all medicine. In the August note he wrote that he has "always believed the No.1 application of AI should be to improve human health", and that he would be "leaning into" Isomorphic Labs. Isomorphic was spun out in 2021 to commercialise AlphaFold's work and has partnerships with Novartis and Eli Lilly. It is now the one company he still runs day to day.

## The open questions

**Did DeepMind's science culture survive Gemini?** The optimistic reading is that AlphaFold finished its job and DeepMind moved its people to the next problems, as Moult and Harvard's Debora Marks argued to *Scientific American*. The less comfortable reading is that a Nobel-winning team was broken up, its best-known member went to a competitor, and the man who built that culture stepped back from running the lab, all within about seven weeks. Both readings fit the facts. Which one was right will show up in whether Google DeepMind produces another AlphaFold-scale result under Kavukcuoglu.

**How much power does a chair have?** Hassabis says he will advise Kavukcuoglu and the Gemini leads and work with Pichai on "strategic and global AGI matters". That is a real job, but it is advisory. The standards body, the Institute and the pacing debate all depend on persuasion, not authority. My read is that this suits him. He has always been at his most effective explaining the science in long interviews and essays. But it also means his influence on what Google actually ships now runs through other people.

**Is the standards body realistic?** The proposal is careful about funding and expertise, the two complaints most often aimed at this summer's ad hoc US government reviews of frontier models. It still needs Congress or the executive branch to bless a new self-regulatory organisation, and other labs to opt in. For now it is a proposal and an argument.

**Isomorphic and Alphabet.** Hassabis is now chief scientist of a public company and CEO of a private Alphabet subsidiary that commercialises some of the same research. The structure is unusual. How the two roles are kept apart is something I would like to see explained more clearly.

## How to read him

Hassabis is still the most scientifically credentialed person at the top of the AI industry, and still the one who most consistently describes AI as a scientific instrument rather than a product. What changed in 2026 is where he applies that. For a decade the answer was "build it carefully inside DeepMind". Now his answer is "build the institutions that decide how everyone builds it", with the standards body, the Institute, the endorsement of Amodei's pacing call and the Dumfries House summit as the first moves.

The bet is the same one he has always made, on a shorter clock. If AGI really is a few years away, the most useful thing the person who solved protein folding can do may be to help set the rules everyone else races by.
