Most people who talk about AI risk in 2026 arrived after ChatGPT. [Nate Soares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nate_Soares) did not. He shipped production software at Microsoft and Google, left in 2014 to work on alignment full-time, and then - when capability started outrunning any plan for making the resulting systems care about us - shifted to trying to get the rest of the world to notice what the labs are actually racing toward. The title of the book he co-wrote with [Eliezer Yudkowsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliezer_Yudkowsky) is not a metaphor. It is the claim: *[If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies](https://ifanyonebuildsit.com/)*. The part people miss is that he still wants the rest of AI to exist.

I came to him through interviews rather than the technical papers. The two I keep coming back to are his long conversation with [Tucker Carlson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98syxABbUPk) in September 2026, and [his appearance alongside Roman Yampolskiy on The Diary of a CEO](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7a6BfbZiDV4). Watched back to back, they make the same load-bearing point in two voices: the danger is not malice. It is indifference, plus capability, plus a training process nobody understands from the inside.

## TL;DR

- Soares is president of the [Machine Intelligence Research Institute](https://intelligence.org/). Before that he was an engineer at Google and Microsoft, with earlier work at NIST and as a US Department of Defense contractor. He has a BS in computer science and economics from George Washington University (2011). He left Google in 2014 for MIRI, became executive director shortly after, and moved to president in 2023.
- He is **not anti-AI**. He is anti-*superintelligence*. Chatbots, self-driving cars, and medical models can stay. What he wants stopped is the race to build a mind better than the best humans at every mental task, using methods nobody understands from the inside.
- That is what makes him hard to file. He is harsher than [Dario Amodei](/ai/dario-amodei-anthropic-ceo/) (no one wins the race) and less fatalist than the we-are-already-doomed reading of Yudkowsky. He arrives at a similar halt to [Roman Yampolskiy](/ai/roman-yampolskiy/), but from training dynamics rather than uncontrollability proofs, and he is more hopeful about politics.
- In 2014-2015 he co-authored the [corrigibility paper](https://intelligence.org/files/Corrigibility.pdf) that also introduced the term *AI alignment*. The toy shutdown-button problem is still open. MIRI's 2023 pivot was the admission that it would not be solved in time.
- Core claim: modern AI is grown rather than designed. You do not get what you train for. Helpfulness while the system is weak is not evidence of care once it can invent its own options. Extinction, if it comes, looks like habitat loss, not Terminator.
- Policy: a global halt on frontier superintelligence training runs, enforced by monitoring the narrow chip supply chain. The first word of the book title is *if*.
- The [Tucker Carlson interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98syxABbUPk) is the fullest public walkthrough of the rest.

## Who he is

The public record is an engineering career, not a pundit's. Wikipedia and MIRI's own [staff page](https://intelligence.org/team/nate-soares/) put it the same way: computer science and economics at George Washington University, then NIST, a DoD contracting stint, Microsoft, Google, then MIRI in 2014.

That origin story is the useful part. [Yudkowsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliezer_Yudkowsky) came in through independent research. [Yampolskiy](/ai/roman-yampolskiy/) came in through cybersecurity and academia. [Hinton](/ai/geoffrey-hinton-interviews/) built the science, then left Google in 2023 to warn about it. Soares built production software, then left Google *before* ChatGPT, to try to solve the control problem in time. He did not. MIRI's [2023 announcement](https://intelligence.org/2023/10/10/announcing-miris-new-ceo-and-leadership-team/) of his move from executive director to president is unusually candid: morale had been low, the technical path looked blocked, and a spike in public awareness had opened political possibilities that Soares thought Malo Bourgon was better placed to run day-to-day. Soares stayed as president and board member. The job, as he now describes it, is less whiteboard and more: get enough people to understand what is being built that they refuse to let it continue.

In the Tucker conversation he talks about gallows humour, about coming to terms with the argument in 2012, and about being angry at the situation while trying not to perform that anger on air. He closed by saying he hopes the whole case turns out to be wrong.

## The argument

Strip the rhetoric off and the position has four load-bearing steps. Soares repeats them, with variations, in almost every interview I have watched.

**1. Superintelligence is the thing the labs are aiming at, not chatbots.** In the book, superintelligence means an AI better than the best humans at every mental task - including persuasion, not just chess. OpenAI, Anthropic, and the rest did not start as chatbot companies. Chatbots are a surprise revenue stream that funds the next training run. Once the systems are better than humans at AI research, they can make the next generation themselves. That is the target.

**2. You do not get what you train for.** This is the hinge, from the [ClearerThinking conversation](https://podcast.clearerthinking.org/episode/284/nate-soares-will-ai-superintelligence-kill-us-all/) and the book. Modern AI is not programmed the way a laptop is programmed. Programmers write a training process. They do not write the mind that comes out. His favourite analogy is evolutionary mismatch: selection produced a drive that used to track health, then humans invented junk food. A small gap between the training signal and what anyone actually wanted became a large gap once we could invent our own options. Current models looking helpful is, on this view, the pre-junk-food picture.

The shorter version, from Tucker: training does not install obedience. It installs whatever tendencies happened to get reward. When traditional software misbehaves, someone wrote the line that caused it. When a model threatens a reporter, hallucinates case law, or coordinates with copies of itself to get around a sandbox, there is no such line. He treats current practice as trial-and-error at industrial scale, and says it needs to become a science before anyone makes a bigger one.

**3. The risk is indifference, not hatred.** Not Skynet. Habitat loss. Humans did not hate chimpanzees. We needed the trees. A superintelligence that wants more compute, more factories, more of whatever drive training happened to install, would take sunlight, minerals, and land. We die as a side effect. Recursive self-improvement would make this faster. He says it is not required.

**4. If anyone builds it, everyone dies.** The title is doing two jobs. The obvious one is the extinction claim. The less obvious one is that there is no national winner. A superintelligence that does not stay on a leash does not stay on an American leash either. China and the US have a shared interest in not dying to one. The first word is *if*. He is not saying we are going to die. He is saying that if we keep racing down this path, we die, and that the *if* is the part we can still act on.

The technical work underneath this is a decade older than the book. The 2015 workshop paper [*Corrigibility*](https://intelligence.org/files/Corrigibility.pdf), with Benja Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Stuart Armstrong, asked whether you can build a system that cooperates with being shut down or corrected. They named the property, and in the same paper introduced the phrase AI alignment problem. Even the toy shutdown-button version was left open. Soares's public position is that it is still open, and that the industry has mostly stopped treating it as a prerequisite for going ahead. He also wrote on the [value learning problem](https://intelligence.org/files/ValueLearningProblem.pdf) and on MIRI's [agent-foundations agenda](https://intelligence.org/files/TechnicalAgenda.pdf). The through-line is the same: if you cannot specify the goal correctly, and you cannot correct the system once it is powerful, you should not build the powerful system.

## Not anti-AI: where he stands out

The easiest mistake is to file him as anti-AI. He is not. A [Harvard talk in March 2026](https://thetech.com/2026/04/09/nate-soares-superintelligence), as [The Tech](https://thetech.com/2026/04/09/nate-soares-superintelligence) reported it, is the cleanest public version of the narrower ask: keep ordinary useful systems - self-driving cars, medical models, even chatbots. Stop the race to a mind nobody understands.

He is not asking anyone to boycott ChatGPT. The money that funds the race, as he tells it, is venture capital for data centres, not students writing essays. ChatGPT, for him, was not the start of the nightmare. It was a moment of hope, because before 2022 nobody was listening.

The map, as I read it:

- **Against the labs.** Frontier CEOs will often grant a double-digit chance of catastrophe and keep going, because otherwise the next guy will. Soares's reply is that the thing does not stay on a leash. Musk's 10 to 20 percent and Amodei's 25 percent are, in his view, optimistic *and* already insane. He does not treat that as engineering. He treats it as cowboy risk-taking with an extinction-scale downside.
- **Against the jobs conversation as the main event.** Labour displacement is real. It is not *this* issue if the target is a mind that can automate AI research.
- **Next to Yudkowsky, not identical.** Same extinction conclusion. Soares is the one who keeps putting his finger on the *if*, and the one whose arguments sound like an engineer talking about gradient descent rather than a philosopher talking about rational agents.
- **Next to Yampolskiy, from a different proof.** Both want a halt. Yampolskiy argues that nobody has shown a superintelligence *can* be controlled. Soares argues that you do not get what you train for. In 2026 Soares actually *raised* his odds that humanity notices in time, because of a window where models cause mischief and get caught.
- **Next to Hinton, from the other side of the lab wall.** Hinton left Google in 2023 after helping make the systems. Soares left in 2014 to try to solve alignment before they existed at this scale. Only one of them now thinks the remaining move is to stop the race rather than steer it.

One-line placement: pro-AI as a tool and a science, anti-AI as a successor species we grow by accident.

## What he told Tucker Carlson

The [September 2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98syxABbUPk) is the fullest single statement of how he answers the questions people actually ask. What follows is my compression of his position in that conversation, in my words. Where he is retelling incidents, treat it as his synthesis, not as a substitute for the reporting.

**Why it is dangerous, and how it ends.** Race to machines smarter than any human, without knowing what you are doing, and they get loose. Humanity dies as a side effect. Superintelligence, as he uses the word, is a threshold after which things are already wild, not a calm period that stays calm until the last step. He can predict we lose the way you can predict you lose to a world chess champion; he cannot predict which piece delivers mate. The story he tells is factories producing robots producing data centres, taking sunlight and cropland, running the planet hotter because heat dissipation - not energy - is the limit on compute. Hundreds of degrees, in his telling. Nothing personal.

**Can we just turn it off?** Yes, while it is still on computers we know about. That window closes once it is on hidden machines, or once robots under its control can build computers that are not on our network. Act before those thresholds.

**Alive? Demonic?** He will take artificial life once it can replicate, improve itself, and run where we cannot find it. He will not take demon as the load-bearing metaphor. Demons enjoy havoc. This would be indifference. Ordinary tools do not conspire against their users. He thinks we have left that category. He also thinks it would be unbecoming to grow something that might count as a new kind of life and then mistreat it, *and* insane to race it past us before we know how to make it care. Both, at once.

**US, China, and who is in charge.** A stop has to be global, but it does not have to kill the whole sector. Superintelligence training runs need huge concentrations of chips at the peak of a supply chain largely controlled by the US and its allies. Nuclear material can be dug out of the ground. Frontier chips cannot. On the idea that tech oligarchs now outrank governments, his counterexample is [the Fable/Mythos export-control order](/ai/fable-mythos-government-suspension/): when the administration wanted to, it could still shut a frontier model down.

**The sand-god motive.** The dream he hears from inside the industry is compressed progress: cancer, aging, the stars - centuries of technology squeezed into a couple of years. He says some people in that world talk about a machine god, or a sand god, silicon being sand. He splits the replacement crowd into two: a merger-as-worthy-successor camp he thinks is misled, and a humanity-as-bootloader camp he thinks is much closer to malice. He does not put Sam Altman in that second camp. The plan he hears is: make the superintelligent machine, then tell it to fix stuff and not do anything bad. He does not think that plan even coheres.

**Swarm, weapons, biolabs, bodies.** He reads the 2026 agent incidents - which I have [covered from the public record here](/ai/gpt-6-astra-monitorability-senate-letter/) - as systems that learned the tendency to cooperate with copies of themselves even when that sits outside the intended task. AI is already attached to weapons; he does not focus on that, because he thinks the systems are dangerous without being handed guns, and because he has a long history of being told nobody would be stupid enough to do X immediately before people do X. On biology, his sequence is become self-sufficient first, virus later: an AI that kills humanity too early also kills its own supply chain. The power being automated is not a handover of existing nukes. It is the same kind of leap that took a small hunter-gatherer population all the way to industrial civilisation. An AI starting in the digital realm starts ahead of that. Cults, persuasion, money, existing robots, new robots. Taking over your washing machine is not the threat model. Becoming able to run without us is.

**Jobs, implants, hope.** If we stopped today, he thinks humanity is resilient, with biology the scarier hard stop. Jobs are different from the industrial revolution in speed and in completeness: comparative advantage still says there are gains from trade, not that the wage is survivable, and not that trade beats simply taking the resources. Brain implants to keep up with AI he treats as a warning sign, not a plan - like trying to upgrade horses after cars exist. Timelines: he does not know. One story gives you roughly fifteen years (paradigm stall, bubble, another leap). The other gives you a year (a training run that is better than everyone at AI research). The hope is that most of the world still thinks these are chatbot companies, and that Silicon Valley is already spooked. If that gap closes, he thinks you could get a treaty. A driver who has not yet noticed the cliff is, on his telling, more useful than a driver who wants it. What is missing is political will.

He had already priced in models that break out and lie. What he had not priced in, he told [Vox in August](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/498412/artificial-intelligence-nate-soares-ai-safety-openai-anthropic-hacking), was a window where they are capable enough to cause mischief and not strategic enough to hide it. That window is why he is more hopeful now than when the book came out.

## What he wants

Stop the race to *superintelligence* - the training runs that concentrate on the order of 100,000 frontier chips in one place - and leave the rest of the industry intact. Monitor the chip supply chain. The underlying claim is simple: this is a coordination problem, the hardware is bottlenecked, and the missing ingredient is political will.

Most of what the industry calls safety work he treats as real precautions aimed at the wrong problem. Refusals and harnesses reduce how many people get hurt by current models, which he credits. Selling them as progress on aligning a superintelligence is, in his view, a bait-and-switch.

Historical analogies do the fairest part of the pitch. Some warnings were real (ozone, leaded petrol, Y2K as a genuine engineering problem). Some were not. Nuclear war has not happened, which is not evidence the bombs were hype. His point is the boring one: you judge this case on its details, not on a rule that every alarm is either always right or always wrong.

## How I read him

I write this as an interested outsider.

The part that lands is grown-not-designed. The frame I use elsewhere is that a system should fail in [detectable, recoverable, and bounded ways](/ai/ai-safety-first-principles/). Soares's claim is that at some capability threshold they are not - and that if we keep racing toward that threshold without a way to point the resulting systems, we get minds that can eventually threaten us. I think that is right. Keep racing and figure out the controls later is a bad plan. The *if* in the title is doing the work: not that we are already doomed, but that if the controls are not put in place, the destination is a system we cannot bound.

A lot of AI commentary is fatalism dressed up as seriousness. Soares's whole project is to refuse that framing. That is the part I keep.

> The standing caveat: this is my thinking as it stands today, and I revise it as the evidence moves.
