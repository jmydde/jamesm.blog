## TL;DR

- AI is making software execution cheaper and faster, which shifts more value toward deciding what is worth building.
- Ideas alone are not scarce. **Judgement** is: seeing a real problem, understanding its constraints, and making a bet that survives contact with customers.
- Deep domain knowledge can become a disproportionate advantage when one person can turn insight into a working product without first assembling a large team.
- The durable moats are less likely to be code and more likely to be proprietary data, relationships, trust, community, and distribution.
- The practical question is changing from "Can we build this?" to "Why should this exist, and why are we the people to make it?"

For most of the software industry's history, a good idea was cheap. The hard part was getting it built.

An idea needed developers, designers, infrastructure, testing, deployment, and maintenance. By the time it became a product, a promising thought could have turned into a six-figure project.

That gap protected execution. If you could repeatedly turn ambiguous requirements into reliable software, you had an advantage that was difficult and expensive to copy.

AI is shrinking the gap. Not to zero, and not for every kind of system, but far enough to change where the constraint sits.

The interesting question is not whether a model can write code. It is what happens when far more people can turn an informed hunch into something users can try.

## Execution is becoming a cheaper input

The old path from idea to product was slow because every stage required specialist labour. A founder had to recruit capability before they could learn whether the original premise was right.

That made early conviction expensive. You needed a plan, a budget, and often an organisation before you had meaningful evidence that anyone wanted the thing.

AI compresses the first loop. A person can outline a workflow, create an interface, model data, draft documentation, prepare a landing page, and put a prototype in front of users in days.

The result is not magic. Production systems still need reliability, security, maintenance, and someone accountable for the outcomes. AI can make a convincing demo quickly; it cannot make a bad product wanted.

But the economic threshold has moved. The useful question is increasingly not "Can I afford a development team?" It is "Can I find out whether this is useful?"

That is a much healthier constraint for experimentation. It replaces a large, speculative commitment with a smaller test of reality.

## Ideas are abundant. Insight is not.

This does not mean that anyone who has an idea wins. If anything, AI makes generic ideas even less valuable.

Ask a model for one hundred startup ideas and it will supply them without hesitation. Ask it for feature lists, business models, names, or market positioning and it will generate more. Brainstorming is now abundant.

The scarce thing is the ability to reject almost all of those ideas for the right reasons.

Real judgement asks harder questions. Is the problem frequent enough to matter? Is it painful enough to change behaviour? Who has the budget or motivation to solve it? What would make the first user return?

It also asks what the prompt did not say. What regulations, habits, incentives, relationships, or awkward edge cases make an apparently elegant product fail in the real world?

That is why the phrase "the people with the best ideas" needs a qualifier. The valuable idea is not a clever concept. It is an insight grounded in a situation you understand better than most people.

In that sense, the important skill is not ideation. It is informed selection.

## Domain knowledge is a source of product vision

Give two people the same models and the same week to build something. Their outputs may look remarkably different.

One sees a blank canvas and asks for a useful app. The other has spent twenty years close to a particular world: its customers, its informal rules, the data people ignore, and the problems nobody has bothered to name.

Take the nightclub industry. Someone who has collected events, DJs, venues, promoters, and historical line-ups can see a map that is invisible to a generalist.

They might recognise which venues incubated a sound, how promoters shaped local scenes, or why a certain DJ matters beyond streaming statistics. They could make that history searchable, connected, and useful.

The model can help create the database, interface, and recommendations. It cannot supply the years of attention that reveal why those connections are worth making.

This is the new leverage. AI gives a domain expert a larger surface area for action. It does not give every outsider the same depth of perception.

The opportunity is not limited to glamorous industries. It exists wherever practitioners carry around detailed knowledge of an inefficient process, an underserved community, or a neglected set of records.

## The advantage is insight plus distribution

The emerging competitive stack is less about having the biggest engineering team and more about combining four things:

| Element | The job it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Insight** | Identifies a problem that genuinely matters. |
| **AI-assisted execution** | Turns the hypothesis into something people can use. |
| **Distribution** | Reaches the people who can validate, adopt, and pay for it. |
| **Defensibility** | Makes the product harder to replace once it works. |

Execution is still necessary. It is simply less likely to be the only scarce input.

Distribution remains stubbornly hard. A better tool that never reaches a customer is still a hobby project. Trust, reputation, communities, partnerships, and an audience cannot be generated as easily as a feature list.

Defensibility matters for the same reason. If a product can be described to an AI, a competitor may be able to recreate a version of it quickly. The code alone becomes a weaker moat.

The more durable assets are the ones that took time to accumulate: proprietary data, a community that trusts you, relationships with users, a strong brand, and a feedback loop that improves the product.

AI can make those assets more useful. It cannot instantly recreate the history that produced them.

## The one-person company gets a serious upgrade

This shift is most exciting for people with unusually specific knowledge and modest initial ambitions.

A specialist no longer needs to begin by raising enough money to employ a complete product team. They can begin with a narrow question: can I make one group's work meaningfully better?

That is likely to produce more niche software, not only more global platforms. The best tool for one kind of analyst, one trade, one community, or one obsessive hobby can become viable at a smaller scale.

These businesses may be uninteresting to a venture fund and immensely valuable to the people who use them. Lower build costs allow the long tail of genuine needs to become a market.

There is a caveat. A one-person company does not become a one-person responsibility. As a product grows, support, security, finance, operations, and customer commitments grow with it.

AI can extend a person's reach. It cannot eliminate the consequences of serving real users. The durable version of this future is a smaller, more leveraged company, not a founder permanently working alone.

## The uncomfortable pressure lands on repeatable work

The difficult implication is that work made up chiefly of repeatable execution is likely to face the sharpest pressure.

If a task is clearly specified and its output can be checked, AI will keep lowering the amount of human effort needed to perform it. That does not mean every job vanishes. It means fewer people may be needed to produce the same output.

This is not an argument that technical skill no longer matters. Good technical judgement remains essential, particularly when systems are complex, risky, or expensive to get wrong.

It is an argument that technical execution alone is no longer a sufficient moat. The differentiating question becomes whether you can uncover the real problem, choose the right trade-off, and take responsibility for the result.

That is true for engineers, but it is just as true for designers, analysts, operators, and founders. The people who merely receive a clear brief are exposed. The people who improve, challenge, or originate the brief gain leverage.

## The question that matters most

For decades, the technology industry treated the ability to build as the final test of an idea. If you could not execute, the idea was mostly irrelevant.

That was rational in a world where execution capacity was scarce. It is becoming less rational in a world where software production is increasingly abundant.

The hard question is moving upstream.

Not: **Can you build it?**

But: **Should anyone build it?**

Answering that well requires taste, context, courage, and contact with reality. It means noticing a problem before it becomes an obvious market category. It means understanding what people value, not merely what they say they want.

AI may make the act of building cheaper for almost everyone. That makes the decision about what deserves to be built more consequential, not less.

The winners will not be people who collect the most generated ideas. They will be people who see something real, use AI to test it quickly, reach the people it matters to, and build an advantage that cannot be copied with the same prompt.
